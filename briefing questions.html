What that means — say this

On the ranking being ours, not generic

Three answers do most of the work. Our agents browse the live web, our coding agents have write access in CI, and teams add third-party MCP servers freely. Those three pushed five capabilities to the top of the scale — supply chain, sandboxing, instruction-channel defence, third-party assessment and coding agents in the SDLC all weight roughly double.

On the headline number

Sixty-one is a distance, not a grade. There's no benchmark behind it — it measures us against current practice, not against peers. It answers what to do first, not how bad we are.

On where it's worst

Agent control and programme governance are tied at thirteen stages each. Agent control is the one I'd lead with, because it's also where our exposure is highest — the gap and the amplification land in the same place.

On the one thing that unlocks the rest

Agent and MCP inventory ranks fifth on its own merits. Structurally, twelve of our thirty-one open items are waiting behind it — including the two with the highest exposure in the estate. If we fund one thing this quarter, that's the one that moves everything else.

On prompt injection, if it comes up

Instruction-channel defence has no top stage — deliberately. Data and instructions share one channel; that's architecture, not a bug waiting for a patch. We contain it downstream by limiting what agents can reach. We don't close it, and nobody else does either.

On the one thing we've reached

Runtime telemetry is our only capability at the frontier, and that's less impressive than it sounds. The ceiling there is stage three because the OpenTelemetry conventions are still moving. We're at the top of a low ceiling, not ahead of the field.

If they ask

How did you get these numbers?
Each capability scored against where practice actually stands today, weighted by how much our deployment makes it matter, divided by effort. Every input is visible — you can argue with any of them.
Is this benchmarked against other companies?
No. It's relative to us. Two organisations with the same answers would get the same numbers, but a score of six isn't a risk level and doesn't compare across firms.
Why is something ranked above something else?
Bigger gap, or it keeps working when other controls fail, or our specific setup amplifies it. Usually all three.
Two rows look near-zero — why score them?
Training data lineage and inference infrastructure barely apply because we're on hosted APIs only. I'd mark both not-applicable, which takes four phantom stages off the sixty-one.
When do we look at this again?
28 October. Several rows are tied to spec and convention changes that move quarterly — the MCP specification and the OpenTelemetry conventions in particular.
